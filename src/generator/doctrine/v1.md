# Progression doctrine — v1

Jack's programming rules, in his words, structured so an engine can run on them. Injected as context for
generation, and recorded by version against every draft so a decision stays explicable later.

Three tiers, and the difference matters:

- **Constraints** are never violated. Code enforces them; the model is not asked to respect them.
- **Principles** must trend the right way. Direction is fixed, magnitude is not.
- **Parameters** are defaults chosen to be adjusted once real output has been looked at.

---

## 1. Constraints

**C1 — Set cap.** In the 6–15 rep range, no more than **4 sets** on one exercise. 6×10 is "a ton" and
never written. 6×6 is still a lot. Observed practice sits at 2–3 sets; 4 is a ceiling approached, not a
target. *(Confirmed against ten real programs: 1,501 prescriptions at 3 sets, 1,400 at 2, only 84 at 4.)*

**C2 — Every working set is hard.** A set counts only if taken near failure: **RPE ≥ 7, or RIR ≤ 3**, which
is the same statement as **relative intensity ≥ 80%**. A set below that is not a working set.

**C3 — Hypertrophy rep range.** 6–30 reps. Below 6 is strength work, not this.

**C4 — One variable at a time.** Something must change every session, and only one thing. Load, reps, or
exercise variation — never two at once. *"We're thinking about this like a science experiment. We have
variables that are constant, and we have variables that change."*

**C5 — Only approved exercises.** Anything prescribed must exist in the library or the coach's own
additions. Enforced by validation, not by asking the model nicely.

**C6 — The ceiling is a load cap, not a difficulty cap.** A block ramps until its last week before the
deload, and what stops it is **how much weight is on the bar**, not how hard the set feels. The cap is
**87.5% of 1RM**. *"If I were doing triples, I definitely would not be going at one hundred percent —
I'd go nothing more than an RPE 9 or 1 RIR... say we're doing sets of twelve, I don't think there's
anything wrong at all going to a 100% relative intensity set by the end of the mesocycle before a
deload, just because the total amount of weight on the bar would have to be significantly less, and
thus injury won't happen."* Both of those fall straight out of the one cap:

| Reps | Hardest legal rung | = % of 1RM | Next rung up would be |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 90% | 85.5% | 88.0% — over |
| 3 | **92.5% (RPE 9 / 1 RIR)** | 85.8% | 88.0% — over |
| 4 | 95% | 85.5% | 87.8% — over |
| 5 | 100% | 87.5% | *nothing above maximal* |
| 6–30 | **100%** | 85% and falling | — |

**Consequence: across the whole hypertrophy range of C3, the ceiling is simply 100%.** The cap only ever
binds at five reps and below, which is strength work. Injury risk is the reason the ceiling exists, and
it is a function of absolute load, which is why the engine can be permissive with high-rep work and must
not be with low-rep work.

**C7 — Below five sessions a week, there is no scheduled deload.** *"I definitely wouldn't deload anybody
who is training anything less than four times a week. Even four times a week, it's not really needed."*
This is the answer to why two real clients ran 41 and 47 weeks unbroken: not a deload hidden somewhere
the analysis missed — there wasn't one. Most of the roster trains 2–4×, so for most of the roster the
answer to "when do we deload" is **we don't**.

**C8 — Rep-count floors, by what the exercise is.** Three tiers, and neither of the lower two was
anywhere in the engine before:

| Exercise | Lowest legal rep count |
|---|---|
| **Accessories** — anything that isn't a compound | **8** |
| Ordinary compounds | 6 |
| **Major lifts** — squat, bench, deadlift, power clean, snatch, front squat, overhead press, hex bar deadlift | **3** |

*"There's pretty much no reason to ever be doing an accessory exercise at anything under the eight rep
range. All accessories should be eight reps and above. The eight rep range and below is pretty much
reserved for your compounds."* And: *"Sets of five and below are only reserved for things like a squat,
bench, deadlift, power clean, snatch, front squat, overhead press, hex bar deadlift, or any major power
exercise that you're trying to move something really fast."*

That last clause matters — the list is the *shape* of the rule, not its whole extent.

## 2. Principles

**P1 — Stress rises every week.** The default direction is up, never "hold". Increase comes from one of
three levers: **a rep**, **a little load**, or **a variation of the same movement**.

**P2 — Small and predictable beats large and surprising.** The body should be able to anticipate the
increase. *"Controlled... somewhat predictable, but not so much that it causes something the body can't
completely expect."*

**P3 — Position in the session decides *which lever*, not just who gets one.** Earlier exercises get more
available energy, so that is where progression should land — and specifically, **early exercises get the
load, late exercises get the reps**. *"In order to get stronger, you have to put more load on the bar.
But what you won't downplay from [adding reps] at all is hypertrophy... So this can be helpful for
exercises after your big heavy hitters, which are usually done earlier in the session."*

**P4 — Novelty is itself a stimulus.** A new exercise carries an acute learning phase — more soreness,
more sensitivity — for a week or two. During it, less intensity is needed on top, because the variation
already spent the week's change. Corollary: a brand-new exercise starts lower on the intensity ramp.

**P5 — The session's total work output is what must rise.** Per-exercise changes serve that. This is a
session-level budget, not independent per-exercise progressions.

**P12 — Dose volume so the muscle heals the day before it is trained again.** This is the rule that says
how *much* work a muscle gets, and it had been missing entirely.

> *"The goal is to try to get the muscle to heal the day before you're gonna train it again. Say you
> train chest on Monday and Friday — on your Monday session you want to do enough volume so that you heal
> on Thursday for your next Friday session, or even Thursday evening if you can get it that perfect. What
> you don't want is to heal later than Friday, and you don't want to heal on Tuesday or Wednesday. If you
> healed on Tuesday or Wednesday, it means you could have added more volume, and thus done more damage,
> and would have spent a whole other day growing."*

**Healing too early is an error, not a margin of safety.** It is days of growth left unbought. So session
volume is a function of the gap to that muscle's next session, and the target is to finish recovering
exactly one day before it.

It also *explains* the frequency targets in section 9 rather than restating them: *"that's why about
twice per week frequency for everything is a good rule of thumb, because it makes it easy spreading those
total sets across two days or three days."*

**P6 — Volume falls modestly as load rises — not proportionally.** Going 4×10 → 4×8 → 4×6 → 4×5 is
40 → 32 → 24 → 20 reps, and that drop is too steep. Some decline is correct and expected; that much is
not. Three things are true at once: sets may flex upward to protect volume, some decline is acceptable,
and part of it is corrected by feel.

**P7 — Reps near failure are worth more.** As a set approaches failure, each rep recruits more motor
units and is more stimulative. So raw rep totals across different intensities are not comparable — 20
reps at 78.8% is not half of 40 reps at 65.3%. This is what licenses P6's decline.

**P8 — Prilepin is a shape, not a budget.** The relationship it describes — as intensity climbs, total
reps must fall — transfers. Its numbers do not: it optimises for strength and bar speed, and its 65–75%
band prescribes 18 total reps against roughly 40 in real hypertrophy practice. Use the direction, ignore
the figures.

**P9 — Pick the two ends, then fill the gaps.** This is the whole progression rule and it is worth
quoting exactly: *"First, when deciding to program, you need to decide where you're gonna start and
where you want to finish. And then you fill in the gaps. So if you've got four weeks, you want to have
appropriate progressions each week such that they're a similar size step forward."*

**The weekly step is an output, not a setting.** It falls out of the two ends and the length. The
previous version of this doctrine had it backwards — it fixed the step at 2.5 pp and derived the start
by counting back from the ceiling, which cannot express a four-week block that spans the full range.

**P10 — A block traverses the entire legal range.** *"I usually like to start blocks around RPE 7 or
RIR 3, and then end the block around RPE 9 or 1 rep in reserve, or even zero reps in reserve."* RIR 3 is
exactly the C2 working-set floor and RIR 0 is exactly the C6 ceiling, so a block is not a segment of the
ladder — it is the **whole ladder, floor to ceiling**, compressed into however many weeks there are.

That is what sets the step. Four weeks across the full span steps 80 → 87.5 → 92.5 → 100, which is
*precisely* the RIR 3 → 2 → 1 → 0 he described in the same breath, arrived at from the ladder alone. It
also fixes the maximum length of an unbroken ramp at **nine weeks** — nine rungs, one a week, P1
forbidding a repeat.

**P11 — The body's reference point is the most recent load, not a maximum.** This is the mechanism under
Model C, and it is why anchoring to last week is correct rather than merely convenient:

> *"Strength is a lot of it a central nervous system adaptation. When you go from thirty fives to
> forties, the percentage load increase is very high even if it's only five pounds. Your body doesn't
> recognize that necessarily. It recognizes what the most recent amount of load you had going through
> that particular vector and direction on your body is... Golgi tendon organs will send an inhibitory
> signal to the muscle fiber to not contract as hard, to protect itself, just because the load on the
> tendon is unfamiliar as in heavy."*

Three things follow directly:

1. **A new load is inhibited on first exposure.** The reps that come out of it are fewer than the
   arithmetic predicts — *"they'd probably only be able to do eight partly because of this."* So the
   first week at a new weight always lands on the band floor, however small the jump looks in pounds.
2. **The rep drop deliberately overpays.** 35 → 40 lb is 14.3%, which the exchange rate prices at 1.7
   reps off a set of twelve. He writes **eight** — a drop of four, more than double. The extra is paying
   for the inhibition, not for the work.
3. **The reference is per movement vector, not per exercise name.** Which is a caution about swapping a
   variation and assuming the load carries: what carries is what that tissue has recently seen.
4. **A detrained person is inhibited everywhere at once.** *"When you're detrained, your GTOs will
   massively limit force output. A lot of the very early gains that you make when somebody first starts
   lifting are central nervous system adaptations and the desensitisation of those GTOs."*

That fourth point has a consequence the engine must not get wrong. A beginner's load will climb fast for
the first weeks, and **it is not evidence the hypertrophy stimulus is well calibrated** — it is
inhibition lifting. So:

- Rapid early progress is expected and should not be read as "the program is working, push harder".
- Nor should it be extrapolated. The rate will fall off when the CNS adaptation is spent, and a plan
  built on the early slope will overshoot.
- P4's learning phase for a *new exercise* is the same mechanism in miniature. A detrained beginner is
  in that state on every exercise simultaneously, which is why their first block is a special case
  rather than a normal block with smaller numbers.

## 3. The three progression models

Both are real and both were given as worked examples. They differ in which variable moves.

### Model A — hold reps, walk intensity up

Four weeks of bench, 4×10 throughout, one step up the relative-intensity column each week:

| Week | Reps | Relative intensity | % of 1RM |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 85.0% | 61.8% |
| 2 | 10 | 87.5% | 63.5% |
| 3 | 10 | 90.0% | 65.3% |
| 4 | 10 | 92.5% | 67.3% |

**Rule: +1 row (2.5 percentage points of relative intensity) per week, reps constant.**

### Model B — walk the load up, walk the reps down

**This is a bridge block, not a hypertrophy block.** Jack's own classification: *"a really good
algorithm to have for a block that is in between a hypertrophy block and a strength block — it's sort of
the bridge in between, where you're transitioning away from the hypertrophy work into the strength
work."* It should not be the default for a general-population client who is not heading anywhere near a
strength phase.

His actual prescription, given in full and corrected from the earlier sketch of it:

| Week | Scheme | % of 1RM | Relative intensity | Total reps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3×10 | 65.3% | 90% | 30 |
| 2 | 3×8 | 72% | 90% | 24 |
| 3 | 4×6 | 76% | 90% | 24 |
| 4 | 4×5 | **83%** | **95%** | 20 |
| 4 *(alt)* | 5×5 | **81%** | 92.5% | 25 |

Three things here were wrong in the earlier record and matter:

1. **Sets are not constant.** They flex *upward* as reps fall — 3, 3, 4, then 4 or 5. This is P6's
   "sets may flex upward to protect volume", with numbers attached for the first time.
2. **The last week escalates.** Difficulty is held at 90% for three weeks and then pushed to 92.5–95%
   at the end. *"You could even make this a little bit higher... increase up to 81 or even 83 percent
   by the end of that same block."*
3. **The volume decline is far gentler than recorded.** 30 → 24 → 24 → 20, not 40 → 32 → 24 → 20. On the
   5×5 ending it is 30 → 24 → 24 → **25**, essentially flat after week 1. P6's "modest, not
   proportional" now has a shape: *one step down early, then hold*.

**The progression here is carried by load, not by difficulty.** *"In terms of relative load on your
joint, you're starting the block at 65% of your 1RM, and by the end you're pushing about 80%."* Which is
why this model needs a different fill rule from Model A.

### The two fill modes

Both models are "pick the ends and fill the gaps"; they differ in *which axis* is spaced evenly.

| | Model A — difficulty mode | Model B — load mode |
|---|---|---|
| Even steps in | relative intensity | % of 1RM |
| The other axis | load follows the table | difficulty follows the table |
| Reps | constant | descend |
| Said out loud as | "RIR 3, then 2, then 1, then 0" | "65% up to about 80% on the joint" |
| Use for | hypertrophy | bridging into strength |

Spacing % of 1RM evenly from 65.3% to 83% across four weeks and snapping each week through the table
returns **65.3 / 72 / 76.5 / 83.3** — against the 65.3 / 72 / 76 / 83 he gave from memory. The model
reproduces his own numbers to within rounding, which is the best evidence so far that the table and the
rule are both right.

*One artifact worth his eye:* the 5×5 @ 81% ending pulls week 2 down to 87.5% relative intensity while
weeks 1 and 3 sit at 90%, so difficulty **dips** mid-block even though load rises every week. The 4×5 @
83% ending does not do this. Whether that dip matters is not yet known.

### Model C — double progression, with the load jump staggered across sets

**This is the one he actually runs on a general-population client, and it never touches a 1RM.** Asked
directly what he looks at to write Carolyn's next session, the answer contained no maximum, no
percentage, and no table lookup — only last week's log.

> *"If I've got three sets of ten with the thirties and they move pretty well, I might say, okay, let's
> either do... two sets of eight with the thirty fives and then one set of eight with the thirties. That
> way, the load increase, even though it's not very small relatively in percentage, is slightly offsetted
> by the volume being ever so slightly lower."*

| Week | Prescription | Tonnage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3×10 @ 30 | 900 |
| 2 | **2×8 @ 35, 1×8 @ 30** | 800 |
| 3 | 2×9 @ 35, 1×9 @ 30 | 900 |
| 4 | 2×10 @ 35, 1×10 @ 30 | 1000 |

**The stagger is the trick worth having.** Moving two of three sets to the next dumbbell is an *effective*
3.3 lb jump out of a rack that only has 5 lb steps — a finer increment than the equipment offers. The
set left behind is what next week promotes.

**The load jump costs a week of tonnage and takes two weeks to earn back.** That dip at week 2 is not an
error; P7 licenses it, because eight reps with the 35s sits closer to failure than ten with the 30s.

**The rep band is what gates the jump.** *"I may, with a client, keep the reps above eight reps at all
times regardless of whether I make a weight jump."* Reps float 8→12; hitting 12 is what earns the load
move; the jump drops the whole exercise — promoted sets and left-behind set alike — back to 8.

### Model C at the top of the band

3×12 with the 35s, and the last set is a fight. What he writes:

| | Prescription | Tonnage |
|---|---|---|
| before | 3×12 @ 35 | 1260 |
| after | **1×8 @ 40, 1×12 @ 35, 1×10 @ 35** | 1090 |

Note what is *different* from the first jump: only **one** set is promoted rather than two, and the sets
left behind **keep their reps** instead of dropping to the floor with it. Resetting all three would have
given 1×8 @ 40 + 2×8 @ 35 = 880, a 30% collapse in a single week. Holding them costs ~13%.

So the two examples disagree about the sets left behind, and Jack said so himself: *"you don't literally
have to do that, but I'm playing around with the reps and the relative intensities."* `holdRemainder`
defaults to holding, because it protects P5 and because this example is the more recent and more
detailed one — but the rule that picks between them is not known. The code also produces 2×12 where he
wrote 12 and 10; that last set is the "playing around", not a rule.

### The exchange rate between the two levers

This is what makes load and reps comparable *without* a 1RM, and it is arithmetic he stated outright:
*"if I just add a rep week over week with the same weight, you're increasing the total workload or stress
by ten percent each set."* One rep on a set of ten is 1/10 of the set.

| Load jump | Cost | = how many reps, at 10 reps |
|---|---|---|
| 30 → 35 lb | 16.7% | 1.7 |
| 15 → 20 lb | **33.3%** | 3.3 |
| 100 → 110 lb machine | 10.0% | 1.0 |

Which is why *"thirty to thirty five is a relatively small jump compared to going from fifteen pounds to
twenty pounds."* **The rack's step is fixed; its relative cost is not, and it is punishing at the light
end** — exactly where general-population accessory work lives. That is the real argument for the
stagger, and for preferring the rep lever late in a session.

### The effort scale, on the same ladder

RPE and RIR are the same axis as relative intensity, and C2 pins both ends of it: 0 RIR is by definition
maximal for that rep count, and RIR 3 / RPE 7 *is* the 80% working-set floor. Three reps in reserve
across twenty points is 6.67 pp each, snapped to real rungs:

| RIR | RPE | Relative intensity |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 10 | 100% |
| 1 | 9 | 92.5% |
| 2 | 8 | 87.5% |
| 3 | 7 | 80% — the floor |
| 4+ | ≤6 | not a working set |

This is what lets a client's logged RPE be read back as a position on the ramp, and what makes "RPE 9"
and "92.5%" the same instruction rather than two competing prescriptions.

## 4. Parameters (defaults, to be tuned against output)

| Parameter | Default | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Where a block opens | **80% RI = RIR 3 = RPE 7** | P10; the C2 floor, stated directly |
| Where a block closes | **92.5–100% RI = RIR 1–0** | P10, capped by C6 |
| Weekly step | **derived** | (start → end) ÷ (weeks − 1), per P9. Not a setting. |
| Longest unbroken ramp | **9 weeks** | Nine rungs floor-to-ceiling, one a week, P1 forbidding a repeat |
| Absolute load cap | **87.5% of 1RM** | C6; reproduces both of Jack's stated ceilings |
| Sets, working range | **2–3**, flexing to 4–5 as reps fall | Observed practice and Model B's 3/3/4/4–5 |
| Volume shape across a block | **one step down, then hold** | Model B: 30 → 24 → 24 → 20–25 |
| Starting relative intensity, new exercise | *unspecified* | P4 says lower — but 80% is already the floor, so "lower" must mean load, not difficulty |
| Rep band (Model C) | **8–12** | "keep the reps above eight reps at all times regardless of whether I make a weight jump" |
| Sets promoted on a load jump | **all but one** | Carolyn's 2-of-3; yields a finer increment than the rack has |
| Reps after a load jump | **the band floor** | The whole exercise resets, promoted sets and left-behind set alike |

## 5. Not yet specified

Recorded so nothing here is quietly invented:

- ~~Whether the **1RM is estimated and stored**.~~ **Answered: it is neither estimated nor stored.** The
  input to next week is last week's log. Model C runs entirely on it, and the relative intensity table
  becomes a *check* on the answer and an *explanation* of it rather than a calculator that produces it.
  Models A and B still speak in %1RM, so they remain coach-facing tools for someone who has a number,
  not the default path.
- **What decides between adding a rep and jumping the load** on a given week. *"It depends on the goal of
  the client, period."* P3 gives the positional half — early exercises take load, late ones take reps —
  but not the rest. Right now the lever is a caller's argument with no rule behind it.
- **Whether the rep band is per-exercise.** 8–12 was given for Carolyn's dumbbell bench. Nothing says
  whether a leg press or a lateral raise floats in the same band.
- **How a block longer than nine weeks works.** P10 fixes the span at floor-to-ceiling and P1 forbids a
  repeated rung, so nine weeks is a hard maximum for one ramp. The presets go to 24 weeks and two real
  clients ran 41 and 47 weeks unbroken. Long blocks must contain internal resets; `fillRamp()` returns
  undefined rather than inventing one.
- ~~**What a deload week actually is.**~~ **Mostly answered by C7:** below 5×/week there is no scheduled
  deload at all, which covers nearly the whole roster. What a deload contains *for someone training 5×+*
  is still unknown, but it is now an edge case rather than a hole in the middle.
- **What decides how many sets get promoted on a jump, and whether the rest hold their reps.** Two
  worked examples, two different answers (2-of-3 with everything reset; 1-of-3 with the rest held).
- **P4 versus P10.** P4 says a new exercise starts lower on the ramp; P10 says every block starts at the
  floor. Both cannot be about the same axis. Most likely P4 means a lower *load* at the same RIR, which
  would be automatic and need no rule — but that is a guess.
- **When to use Model A versus Model B** for a given person, given that Model B is explicitly a bridge
  into strength work rather than a hypertrophy progression.
- The **volume budget per intensity band** in hypertrophy terms — Prilepin's column rewritten.
- **Exercise selection**: what picks lat pulldown over a row for a given person.
- **Session order**: whether there is a rule beyond "big things early, core late".
- **Swap triggers**: what takes an exercise out mid-block.

## 6. Where the textbook sits (CSCS, ch. 17 & 21)

Pulled from *Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning*, chapter 17. Recorded because the
divergences are deliberate and should not be quietly "corrected" by anyone reading this later.

| | CSCS, hypertrophy | This practice |
|---|---|---|
| Load | **67–85% 1RM** (table 17.9) | ~65% at ten reps, 90% RI — *below the floor* |
| Reps | **6–12** (17.9, 17.11) | 10–20 routinely, occasionally 30 |
| Sets | **3–6** (17.11) | **2–3**, capped at 4 |
| Rest | **30 s – 1.5 min** (17.12) | 1:30–2:30 in the real programs |
| Frequency | beginner 2–3, intermediate 3–4, advanced 4–7 (17.4) | 2–4 |

**The divergence is consistent in one direction: lighter, longer sets, fewer of them, more rest.** That
is a defensible read of the population — CSCS is written for athletes, and these are middle-aged
general-population clients where joint cost and session tolerance matter more than squeezing the last
percent of hypertrophic stimulus. It is recorded here as a choice, not an oversight.

Where they agree, and which is therefore safe to encode as shared ground:

- **Exercise order**: power first, then multi-joint / large muscle, then single-joint / small muscle
  assistance. "Power exercises require the highest level of skill and concentration of all the exercises
  and are most affected by fatigue." This matches P3 and matches the ten real programs, which open with a
  squat or pull pattern and finish with core.
- **Sets rise with training age**: "It is appropriate for an athlete to perform only one or two sets as a
  beginner and to add sets as he or she becomes better trained." Sets are a function of what someone can
  tolerate, not a fixed prescription.
- **Load and reps are inversely related**, which is the whole basis of the relative intensity table.

### The load increment is not constant, and the table already knows it

One rung of relative intensity (2.5 percentage points), expressed as load:

| Reps | Δ %1RM per rung | On a 300 lb 1RM |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 2.5 pp | 7.5 lb |
| 10 | 2.0 pp | 6.0 lb |
| 20 | 1.0 pp | 3.0 lb |

Across the full 80→100% ladder: 18.5 pp of 1RM at three reps, 14.5 at ten, 9.0 at twenty.

So a fixed "+5 lb a week" rule is wrong at both ends. **The engine works in relative intensity and never
in pounds**, then snaps the result to what the equipment can actually do — `stepForEquipment` already
gives 5 lb for dumbbells and barbells, 10 for machines and cable stacks. A computed 82.4 lb has to become
an 85 lb dumbbell, because that is what exists on the rack.

## 7. Building week one

Everything above is week two onward — given last week's log, write next week. This is the other half.

### The split

**Full body, every day, with a rotating lead.** *"I would definitely give them a full body split. On the
first day you're starting with a chest exercise, the second day a back exercise, and the third day a leg
exercise. But each day you still do a little bit of full body."*

### The session shape

Top down, by importance. *"What's the most important? Compounds. Bang for your buck."*

| Slot | What goes there |
|---|---|
| 1 | The day's lead compound — chest / back / legs by day |
| 2 | A second compound, **a different body part from the lead** |
| 3 … n−2 | Accessories, "smaller body parts" |
| last 2 | *"Really small body parts, like abs or forearms or calves"* |

**No more compounds after slot 2.** *"You don't really need much more compound lifting unless you're
really trying to lift like a bodybuilder, and this specific person and demographic doesn't really need
that."*

### The lead exercise, per day

| Day | Lead | Candidates |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chest | Dumbbell or barbell press of some type — **or a push-up if they aren't strong**, and progress that |
| 2 | Back | A pull-up variation (assisted / bodyweight / pulldown) **if they want to work toward a pull-up**; otherwise **a row that is not chest supported**, or a hex bar deadlift |
| 3 | Legs | A squat |

Note both conditionals: the lead is chosen against the *person*, not the slot. Someone who can't press
gets a push-up as their week-one chest compound and the progression runs on that.

### The back taxonomy

Stated outright, and unusually tidy. *"With back exercises, there's only two types. There's vertical
pulling and horizontal pulling, and then your spinal erectors having to resist spinal flexion, which you
can take out of it if it's chest supported. Those are the only three types of back exercises you can
do."*

So **erector loading is a flag on a movement, not a pattern of its own.** A bent-over row and a
chest-supported row are the same horizontal pull; they differ only in whether the erectors work. Which is
exactly why he specifies "not chest supported" when he wants that stimulus, and why the day-two lead
carries a `wantsErectors` flag.

### The second slot

His stated rule is only "not the same body part as the lead." His worked week resolves the rest:

| Day | Lead | Second | His reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | incline dumbbell press | vertical pull | different body part |
| 2 | unsupported row | **flat** press | a second, different press variation |
| 3 | squat | vertical pull | *"we haven't done one since day one"* |

So: **push day takes a pull, pull day takes a push, leg day takes whatever has gone longest untrained**
— and within the winning category, the sub-pattern longest unscheduled, which is what steers day one
onto the *vertical* pull since the horizontal one is already day two's lead.

**Why day two takes a second press rather than the untouched overhead press:**

> *"There's nothing wrong with overhead pressing, but I don't like it very much, because it primarily
> just works the anterior deltoid, which gets a lot of work from regular chest work — especially incline
> pressing, which will get your upper chest. And it's not going to cosmetically change that much. It's
> more of a very strength-based exercise. If I was working with an athlete, we might be doing some
> shoulder presses if he's a basketball player and he needs strength in that specific motion. But the
> chest is just of much more importance."*

**The exclusion is cost-benefit, not safety** — which makes the exception precise. Restore overhead
pressing when the goal is sport strength in that motion; not because a client is younger or healthier.
With it excluded the planner reproduces his week on all six compound slots.

### The first weight

There isn't one, and that is the answer rather than a gap:

> *"First weight, just have them pick up something super light so they can feel the motion, to where it's
> decently difficult. Probably an RIR of five. But you're never going to encounter somebody like this
> very often. I wouldn't even worry about the weight, to be honest. I would just make them feel
> comfortable doing the exercise and understanding the motion, and then getting comfortable with it
> eventually to where they can make it a little bit heavier."*

So week one's load is **deliberately below the C2 working-set floor** — RIR 5 is not a working set, and
is not meant to be. The first session buys motor learning and a logged number to anchor to, nothing else.
Which also means the generator should not try to compute a starting load at all: **it should leave the
box open and let the first session fill it.** P11 says the same thing from the physiology — a detrained
person is inhibited everywhere, so any number computed for them would be wrong anyway.

### Exercises per session

He said "somewhere between nine and twelve", and *"if you're not planning on supersetting any exercises
together, which wouldn't happen until the later half of the workout anyway."*

His own ten programs say nine-to-twelve is the **two-day** number. The count falls as frequency rises:

| Days/week | Mean exercises per session | Clients |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | **9.8** | Carolyn, Marco, Teressa |
| 3 | **8.4** | Arish, Vanessa, Wasiqua, Jing |
| 4 | **6.8** | Bella, Colin |

Which is the sensible reading — fewer sessions, more work in each. Defaults are set from the data rather
than the quote.

## 8. His own training — the advanced case

`JACK 6X.xlsx`, eight sheets, four lettered blocks, 41 sessions, six days a week. This is the only
sample of what he writes for a *serious* lifter, and it is his own, so the intent behind every choice is
first-hand rather than inferred.

### Blocks are short, named by phase, and run continuously

| Block | Weeks | Phase |
|---|---|---|
| A | 4 | PRE-CUT (4/6 – 5/4) |
| B | 2 | PRE-CUT (5/4 – 5/31) |
| C | 5 | SUMMER 1 |
| D | 2+ | SUMMER 2 |

Two to five weeks, dated, named for the goal rather than a number. **No deload sheet anywhere** — at six
days a week, above C7's threshold. The block boundary appears to do that job instead.

### The block boundary is an exercise swap

This is the strongest single finding in the file:

| | Shared exercises |
|---|---|
| Within block A (wk 1-2 → wk 3-4) | **88%** |
| Within block C (wk 1-2 → wk 3-4) | **84%** |
| A → B | **16%** |
| B → C | 53% |
| C → D | **19%** |

**Inside a block you keep the exercises and move the numbers. At the boundary you throw most of them
out.** That is P4 — novelty as a stimulus — operating at the scale of a whole program rather than one
lift, and it is a concrete answer to "what takes an exercise out": the block ending does.

**And it holds for clients, not just for him.** Re-reading the long client programs for turnover rather
than for content:

| | Consecutive weeks | Week 1 vs last week |
|---|---|---|
| Marco, 41 weeks | **88%** mean | **0%** |
| Jing, 43 weeks | 76% mean | 5% |
| Carolyn, 6 weeks | 92% mean | 67% |

High week-to-week continuity, and **complete turnover across a year** — not one exercise survives from
Marco's first week to his last. Marco's churn is concentrated at two points (weeks 3 and 39) with 88%
continuity elsewhere, which is the same block-boundary shape; Jing's is spread over eight low-overlap
weeks, closer to rolling replacement. So a long "block" in the app is a chain of shorter ones whose
seams are exercise swaps — which is the answer to how a 24-week preset works, arrived at from the data
rather than by asking.

### The session has three zones, not a gradient

He tiers his own exercises T1…T7 explicitly, which is "top down by importance" written on the page.
Across 41 sessions:

| Slot | Mean reps | Mean sets | Mean rest |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **5.6** | 3.1 | 98 s |
| 2 | 10.4 | 2.7 | 72 s |
| 3 | 10.6 | 2.1 | 45 s |
| 4–5 | ~11 | ~2.3 | — |
| last | **20.6** | 2.1 | — |

**One heavy low-rep opener, a long middle around ten to twelve, one high-rep finisher.** Sets and rest
both fall monotonically down the session. The 5.6-rep opener is an advanced lifter's number and sits
below C3's hypertrophy floor — the *shape* transfers to a general-population client, the absolute reps
do not, which is what `strengthBias` interpolates.

### Which lever moves, week to week

Across every exercise where both weeks are legible (his weeks are paired **BASE** then **LOAD**):

| Change | Share |
|---|---|
| Load up, reps the same | **39%** |
| Identical | 17% |
| Reps up, load the same | 16% |
| Both up | 10% |
| Load up, reps down | 6% |

**55% of exercises take load, 16% take reps** — the reverse of what Model C does for Carolyn, and exactly
what P3 predicts once you notice that a serious lifter's session is mostly heavy compounds.

*(The 17% that repeat unchanged were queried and are **a spreadsheet error**, not a programming choice —
his words: "that's an error, don't worry about that." Recorded so nobody re-derives a hold rule from
them. P1 stands: stress rises every week.)*

### Exercises per session, corrected

His own program runs **5.5 exercises a session** at six days a week. Against the client data:

| Days/week | Per session | Weekly total |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 9.8 | 19.6 |
| 3 | 8.4 | 25.2 |
| 4 | 6.8 | 27.2 |
| **6** | **5.5** | **33.0** |

His own caution about reading this as a rule: *"definitely don't measure [it] for the amount of days per
week, because that just goes with how serious you are about training."* The count per session falls and
the weekly total climbs, but the causal variable is commitment, not frequency. So it seeds a default and
the caller overrides it.

## 9. Frequency targets, the budget, and the swap rate

### The targets

> *"Pretty much the goal each week for each person is that every muscle gets trained at least two times.
> At least two, and at most four — four is pushing it. But for muscles that are much more of a priority,
> ideally three, assuming they're training five or six days a week, maybe even four."*

**Men** — *"guys: pretty much mandatory, have to train chest twice per week, have to train back twice per
week, have to train legs at least once per week, ideally two. Biceps, triceps and shoulders at least two
times per week. Some guys don't care about abs, but if you do, somewhere between two and four times a
week at most. For hamstrings, at least once per week. For calves, they don't have to unless they really
want to — same with traps and forearms."*

**Women** — *"For girls, I always have at least two leg days — a lot of them want three. And what that
entails is two of the leg days being very glute focused, so glute exercises early on. Usually one of them
is some type of a hip thrust, or a heavy lunge or a squat, and then some RDLs. Back at least twice per
week, chest once per week, maybe twice if they like it. And then biceps, triceps and shoulders — usually
once a week, maybe twice if they really want to."*

| | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| Chest | **2** | 1, up to 2 |
| Back | **2** | **2** |
| Legs / quads | 1, ideally 2 | **2**, often 3 |
| Glutes | — | **2**, ideally 3, *early in the session* |
| Hamstrings | 1 | 2–3 |
| Biceps / triceps / shoulders | **2** | 1, up to 2 |
| Abs | 2–4, if they care | if they care |
| Calves / traps / forearms | only if they want them | only if they want them |

Recorded as he said it — "guys" and "girls" — but modelled as an **emphasis profile**, because the
variable actually doing the work is what the client wants trained. An intake preference overrides the
default, so a woman who wants to bench heavily gets that rather than a glute program she didn't ask for.

### The budget

Targets are what to aim for; the schedule is what can be afforded. Asking for *everything*, including
every optional muscle, and seeing what the budget cannot buy:

| Schedule | Gives up |
|---|---|
| 2×/week | obliques, traps, calves, forearms, front delts, adductors |
| 3×/week | front delts, adductors |
| 4×+ | nothing |

Which is his own description of the trade: *"if you've got six days a week you can spend a lot of time on
forearms, traps, calves… twice a week you're not gonna be doing much posterior deltoid, lower traps. Some
things will be neglected."* `neglectedAt()` returns the list, and it belongs in the UI at
program-creation time — choosing two days a week is choosing what to neglect.

### Where the targets and the programs disagree

Days per week each muscle is actually trained, across nine client programs (45 client-weeks of men, 101
of women):

| Muscle | Men | target | Women | target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chest | 1.91 | 2 | 1.89 | 1–2 |
| Back | 2.09 | 2 | 2.34 | 2 |
| Quads | 1.98 | 1–2 | 2.34 | 2–3 |
| Glutes | **0.00** | — | **1.27** | 2–3 |
| Biceps | 1.93 | 2 | 1.72 | 1–2 |
| Triceps | 1.33 | 2 | 1.89 | 1–2 |
| **Hamstrings** | **0.29** | 1 | **0.45** | 2–3 |
| **Rear delts** | **0.00** | 2 | 0.28 | 1–2 |

Chest, back, quads and biceps land on target, and the glute split is exactly as described — zero for the
men, 1.27 for the women. Two places diverged:

- **Hamstrings** — 0.29 against a target of 1 for men, 0.45 against 2–3 for women.
- **Rear delts** — 0.00 for the men against a stated target of two.

**Both were put to him. His answer: *"those are just errors on both of those ends."*** So the targets
stand and the programs were the mistake. A generator built on these numbers will write more hamstring and
rear delt work than his own files contain, and that is the point rather than a divergence to preserve.

Worth recording as the one case so far where the **data was wrong and the stated rule was right** — the
default assumption running through this document is the reverse, and a later reader might otherwise
"correct" these targets back down to match the files.

### Training age sets the swap rate

This resolved what looked like a contradiction: he rebuilds his own program every 2–5 weeks, but Marco's
41 weeks contain only two rebuild points.

> *"Most of these people that are my clients are mostly beginners. When they came to me I got a lot out
> of them really fast and I didn't need to swap exercises around much — they could just keep making
> gains for almost a full year on end. That's usually how it is when you're in your first year or two of
> training. It's called newbie gains... As somebody starts becoming a bit more intermediate, they're
> gonna start plateauing on things a lot sooner, which requires variation with exercises that will
> correlate to each other."*

**Same coach, different training age.** And it agrees with P11 from the physiology: a detrained beginner
is inhibited on everything, their first year is largely that inhibition lifting, and inhibition lifting
does not need novelty to keep going. An advanced lifter has spent it, and novelty is most of what's left.

| Training age | Swap interval | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Beginner (< 2 yr) | **20 weeks** | *"almost a full year"*; Marco's two seams in 41 weeks |
| Intermediate (2–7 yr) | 8 weeks | interpolated — the least confident number here |
| Advanced (7 yr +) | **4 weeks** | his own blocks, 2–5 weeks, 81–84% of exercises replaced at each seam |

And a swap is not a free choice: *"variation with exercises that will correlate to each other."* The
replacement is the same movement pattern in a different expression — incline for flat, dumbbell for
barbell, pulldown for pull-up. **The pattern is the invariant across a swap**, which is what `patternOf`
is for.

## 10. Two things the logs can tell you

### Why reps descend across sets, and what the size of the fall means

82% of the multi-set rep entries in the client programs descend — `12,10,8`, `11,8,6`. The question was
whether that is prescribed or recorded. **It is recorded, and it is C2 working correctly:**

> *"There's nothing wrong with having a descending rep scheme at all. You can either have it be three
> sets of ten, you can have it be twelve ten eight — it doesn't matter how you group the sets... If you
> do three sets of ten with the same weight, the first set isn't going to be as hard as the last one.
> When you have a descending rep scheme of somewhere around two or three reps off each set, it mostly
> happens because the relative intensity of each set is really high. They may be training really close
> to failure on that first set, and because of that they're not fully recovered by the second set, and
> therefore they only do ten reps instead of twelve."*

If every set is genuinely near failure, reps **must** fall. So the size of the fall is a free diagnostic
out of data a client already logs. Measured across 2,237 multi-set entries in eight client programs:

| Consecutive-set drop | Share | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **16%** | the first set wasn't close enough to failure |
| 1 | 17% | |
| **2** | **33%** | |
| 3 | 12% | *his stated 2–3 band — 45% of all pairs* |
| 4+ | 14% | set one was taken too far and the rest paid for it |

Median drop **exactly 2**, mean 1.89. His "two or three reps off each set" is the real distribution, not
a rule of thumb. `judgeSetFatigue()` reads it back.

**Consequence for the data model:** prescribed reps are a target and the per-set actuals are the
measurement — which `SetPrescribed` / `SetActual` already separate correctly. The generator should write
one target, not a descending scheme, and let the descent happen.

### What makes a swap a real swap

The advanced lifter's need for variation has two causes, and they want different things:

> *"Because I'm very advanced I need to change stimulus often, or else I will either hit a plateau on my
> strength gains, or my connective tissues — as in my tendon complex — will start getting irritated from
> the exercise, because I'm really strong... Variation is important because it has a **different wear
> pattern on the tendon** even if it works the same muscles. There's a difference between a hack squat
> and a leg press even though you both have knee flexion and hip flexion. Even with identically the same
> percentages of hip and knee flexion it still isn't the same, because the direction of the load is
> placed differently, and the times at which the load is most stressful are different. **The torques are
> different.**"*

A **plateau** wants a novel stimulus. **Tendon irritation** wants a different loading profile through the
same range. A swap that keeps the muscle and the joint angles but changes neither equipment nor angle of
resistance solves neither.

**So a valid swap keeps the pattern and changes the torque curve.** The pattern is what the slot is for;
the torque curve is the entire point of swapping. `isValidSwap()` enforces both halves and gives a reason
either way. The real quantity is a resistance curve through the range of motion, which nothing in the
library records — equipment class plus angle cues is the available proxy, and it does separate his own
example.

**This is also the tendon argument for why an advanced lifter swaps sooner** — not that they get bored,
but that they are strong enough for the same wear pattern to accumulate into irritation. It belongs
alongside the training-age swap intervals in section 9, and it is a second, independent reason for the
same rule.

## 11. The volume rule, and what the app already collects

P12 is the first rule in this doctrine that sets **how much** work a muscle gets. Everything before it
set how hard (relative intensity), how often (frequency targets), or in what order (session shape).

| Chest on Mon and Fri — gap of 4 days | Reading |
|---|---|
| Heals Tuesday or Wednesday | **Too little volume.** One or two days of growth left unbought. |
| Heals **Thursday** | Correct. |
| Still sore Friday | **Too much volume.** More damage than the gap can absorb. |

### The app is already collecting the input

`computeSorenessDue()` in `src/shared/soreness.ts` works out which muscles a session is about to train
that were trained recently, and how many days ago; the client answers 1–5, where 1 is wrecked and 5 is
fully healed. That module's own comment already guessed at this — *"the real signal for whether that
muscle's volume is set too high"* — without a rule to read it against. P12 is the rule, and
`recoveryWindow.ts` is the reading.

### One question would make it work properly

There is a real limit in what today's check-in can tell you. A score of 5 on session day means the muscle
healed **at some point** in the gap — which is the on-target case and the healed-too-early case both, and
a single reading cannot separate them. Only the failure at the far end (still sore) is unambiguous.

**Distinguishing "healed Thursday" from "healed Tuesday" needs the client to say *when* the soreness
stopped.** That is one extra question on a check-in they already answer, and it is the single
highest-value thing that could be added to the app for this engine — it converts an ambiguous reading
into a volume prescription for every muscle, every week, automatically.

### The response is asymmetric, because the errors are not

> *"We want to edge on the side of caution, because too much each week can result in injury. If you're a
> little bit sore and you train once like that, it's fine. But it's bad when it happens repeatedly —
> mostly because if you're training when you're not fully healed, you still have damage done to the
> muscle and then you redo the damage again. But it also puts a lot of extra stress on the tendon
> complex, since **the tendon complex already takes longer to heal than the muscle belly itself.** That's
> partly why exercises need to be swapped for individuals. That's why we also ask about joint pain — for
> that reason, and previous injuries, and joint tightness can contribute as well."*

Under-dosing costs a day of growth. Over-dosing **compounds**, and it compounds onto the slowest-healing
tissue in the joint. So:

| Reading | Response |
|---|---|
| Still sore, once | **Cut a set immediately** |
| Recovered early, once | Hold — one reading is noise |
| Recovered early, twice running | Add a set |
| Still sore, **three sessions running** | Cut *and* **swap the exercise** |

That last row is the important one. By the third consecutive unhealed session this has stopped being a
volume problem and become a tendon problem, and the remedy is section 10's swap — same pattern, different
torque curve, different wear pattern — not fewer sets on the same movement.

**And this is why the joint-pain question exists.** The soreness check measures the muscle; the joint
question measures the tissue that heals slower and accumulates quietly. Together with previous injuries
and joint tightness from the intake, they are the inputs to the same decision.

Adjustments stay at one set at a time in both directions. P2 applies to volume as much as to load, and a
single soreness reading is noisy — a bad night's sleep moves it as much as mis-set volume does.

## 12. Warm-ups

Percentages of the **working load**, not of 1RM — the same choice Model C makes, and for the same reason.

> *"I really like to have about half as much as your working load for about 10 reps, then about 75% of
> your working load for another 5 to 8 reps, then a little bit more weight for a single or a double, and
> then I go for my working set from there."*

| Rung | Load | Reps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **50%** of working | 10 |
| 2 | **75%** | 5–8 |
| 3 | a bit more | 1–2 |

His own, working to 3×8 with the 100 lb dumbbells on flat press: **50×10, 75×5–8, 85×1–2, 95×1.** Four
rungs rather than three, and he says why — *"this is a little bit repetitive as it's just a high total
number of weight, so I like to do singles and doubles for me specifically."* **More absolute load on the
frame means more rungs**, and the extra ones go in near the top: it is the singles and doubles that get
denser as the weight climbs, not the light work.

### The count is set by the body part, not the exercise

This is the part that is invisible if you only look at one exercise at a time.

> *"That goes for the first of the first exercise for that body part. So say I'm doing another chest
> exercise after that — I might do one, maybe two at the most, with a similar progression of jumping up
> in load. The only thing I would spend more time warming up for are bigger exercises. If I was going to
> bench press heavy that day I'd have a different warm-up scheme, and if I was going to squat, I'd
> probably do four warm-up sets."*

| Position | Rungs |
|---|---|
| First exercise for that body part | **3** |
| Any later exercise for the same body part | **1**, at most 2 |
| A big lift — heavy bench, any squat | **4** |

### Warm-up loads round *down*

Never up: a warm-up that lands heavier than intended is the one direction this must not err in. His client
example shows the same instinct — working up to 75, he opens at 30 rather than the 37.5 that half would
be, and 35 is the nearest thing a rack actually has.

The ramp also collapses rather than repeating itself. A 10 lb lateral raise gets one warm-up with the 5s,
because 7.5 is not a dumbbell that exists and every higher rung snaps back onto the same weight.

### What a warm-up is actually for

The mechanism, which explains the rules above rather than just accompanying them:

> *"The whole goal of warming up is to stretch out the tissue a little bit so that it's warm —
> importantly, because warm things are pliable — but also to take the muscle through the range of motion
> that it's going to be worked in, and then gradually increase the activation of the nervous system, so
> that by the time you hit your first set those motor units are already a bit more stimulated and primed
> for your working sets. **But the most important thing is that the muscle is warm in temperature and
> flexible.**"*

Three jobs, and they are **not spread evenly across the rungs**:

| Rung | Buys | Why it looks like it does |
|---|---|---|
| 50% × 10 | **temperature + range** | needs volume and full range, not load — and is the one never to cut |
| 75% × 5–8 | the transition | |
| 85–95% × 1–2 | **nervous-system priming** | singles and doubles near the working weight; more absolute load wants more of them |

**This is what explains the per-body-part rule.** Once the tissue is warm and has been through its range,
a second exercise for the same muscle does not need to buy that again — only priming for a movement the
nervous system hasn't done yet today, which is one rung near the working load.

### Which lifts get the long ramp

> *"Any of the big compounds — bench, squat, deadlift, any sort of Olympic lift, even some like super
> heavy barbell rows or T-bar rows or cable rows. These require a little bit more warming up, just
> because the loads on them especially can get really heavy and they use a lot of different muscles, so
> you need to make sure that you feel good going into them and that you're ready to go."*

Two criteria in that sentence, and they split the list in two:

- **Always four rungs** — squat, bench, deadlift, Olympic lifts, overhead press, hip thrust. Loaded by
  definition; no amount of earlier chest work primes the nervous system for 225 on a bar.
- **Four only when it opens the body part** — barbell, T-bar, Pendlay and cable rows. His qualifier was
  *super heavy*, and these are very heavy in his own training and ordinary accessory work in a client's.
  A cable row arriving after a heavy barbell row gets two rungs, not four.

*Not specified:* whether a **dynamic warm-up** precedes any of this. *"I can either have people do a
dynamic warm-up beforehand, which I'm not usually going to do. I might do that later."* Recorded as
deliberately out of scope rather than forgotten.

## 13. Rest, and supersets

### Rest is a band, and it is buying something specific

> *"I usually don't go too crazy about rest times — it's usually when the other person is ready."*

But the bounds are not arbitrary, and the reason for the long end is physiological:

> *"In your first two exercises, maybe your first three, those are the ones you're trying to get pretty
> strong on, so you want rest to be a little bit longer. I usually err on the side of a minute thirty at
> the lowest to three minutes, or three thirty even on occasions. **The reason for that is the ATP
> regeneration between sets — for strength specifically, stressing your ATP-creatine phosphate system. It
> takes about 3 to 5 minutes to fully replenish it, so somewhere around two, two and a half is around
> 80%, which is decent.**"*

| Where | Who | Band | Default |
|---|---|---|---|
| First 2 exercises | wants to get strong | **1:30 – 3:30** | 2:30 |
| First 2 exercises | doesn't care about strength | 0:45 – 2:00 | 1:30 |
| Everything after | accessory muscles | **1:00 – 1:30** | 1:15 |

*"As the session goes on the rest can come down a little bit — around a minute to a minute and a half,
for smaller muscles that don't require as much energy and are more accessory-type muscles."*

**This corrected a number I had wrong.** The slot profile taken from his own 6× program had the tail at
45 seconds; his stated floor for accessory work is 60. The measured number was his own training, which is
denser than what he writes for a client.

### Supersets

Optional, and for volume rather than strength.

> *"I wouldn't do it if somebody's trying to get really strong on an exercise, but **it's a fantastic way
> of adding volume.** I usually like to do them later in the lifts... after your first two exercises,
> which are usually your heavy hitters."*

Two hard edges — **never in the opening slots, never on a lift someone is trying to get strong on** — and
one purpose. It also explains a number from section 7: exercises per session is 9–12 *"if you're not
planning on supersetting any exercises together"*. Pairing raises what fits in the same hour.

| Pairing | Example | Style |
|---|---|---|
| **Antagonist** | biceps + triceps | bodybuilding |
| **Adjacent** | biceps + forearms | bodybuilding |
| **Same muscle** | flies + push-ups | *his favourite, last in the session only* |
| **Circuit** (3+, repeated) | pull-up + tricep pushdown + band external rotation | strength & conditioning |

Who it suits: *"pretty normal in strength and conditioning — if somebody wants general fitness and they
want to be a little bit more athletic, have a little more endurance, be a little more conditioned."*

Unrelated exercises are left unpaired rather than forced together — two things back to back with nothing
in common is a rushed session, not a superset.

**Rest inside a superset is zero.** *"You don't rest in between sets of a superset. That's why it's
called a superset."* So the rest figures above apply **once per round**, not once per exercise — which is
most of why pairing buys volume without buying time, and why exercises per session is 9–12 only *"if
you're not planning on supersetting"*.

## 14. The rep zones

*"Eight to twelve for everything is not, uh, true at all."* Asked four times whether one band covered
everything; it does not, and here is the map.

| Zone | Reps | What it's for |
|---|---|---|
| **Peak strength** | 1–5, *mostly 1–3* | Below C3's floor. C6's load cap binds here — a triple stops at RPE 9. |
| **Middle strength** | 3–6 | Where Model B's bridge block finishes. |
| — | **7** | **A deliberate gap.** Belongs to neither. |
| **Lower hypertrophy** | **8–12** | *"A gold standard for hypertrophy, which is a very common goal for most individuals."* |
| **Middle hypertrophy** | 12–15 | |
| **Upper hypertrophy / muscular endurance** | 15–20 | Where his own sessions finish — the last slot averages 20.6 reps. |
| **Cluster / metabolic / endurance** | 20–30 | *"Reserved pretty much strictly for cluster sets, or crazy forearm training, or calf raises."* |

**Endurance is not a seventh zone — it is a second quality the top two already buy.** *"Add to the upper
hypertrophy range as muscular endurance. And metabolic as well is definitely some endurance, that being
the twenty–thirty rep range."* Which means a client who wants conditioning does not need a different
program, only the back half of the session written higher.

**Seven belongs to no zone — which is not the same as being forbidden.** An earlier version of this
document had it as a rep count he avoids, and that was wrong: his ascending hypertrophy block runs 3×6,
**3×7**, 4×8, 4×10. *"Discluding seven"* was drawing a zone boundary, not banning a number. A set of seven
is an ordinary thing to prescribe in transit; it just isn't the name of a training zone.

Where the bands overlap at their edges, the **lower** zone wins. Otherwise a triple lands in middle
strength rather than peak strength, and a set of twelve falls outside the gold standard — both of which
contradict the way he said it.

## 15. Warm-ups, corrected and completed

### The counts

| Lift | Rungs |
|---|---|
| Bench, squat, deadlift, Olympic lifts, **military / shoulder press, heavy dumbbell presses** | **4** if it's the first thing that day, otherwise 3 |
| Rows — barbell, T-bar, Pendlay, cable | **3** |
| A big lift arriving on an already-warm body part | 2 |
| Anything else after the body part is warm | 1 |

*"Rows, three. Bench, squat, deadlift get at least three, or even four with a single — especially if they
are the first thing that day."* Which corrected two things I had wrong: rows were getting four, and the
fourth rung was tied to the lift rather than to being first in the session.

### Accessories ramp differently, and finish at the working weight

*"For the warm-up scheme for accessory exercises — pretty much anything that's not a compound lift, not
heavily multi-joint; simple hinges and simple extensions, things that don't involve triple extension or
very heavy hip hinging — the parameters I gave above, including singles and doubles, are mostly reserved
for the lifts I'm talking about."*

His worked example, 3×10 lateral raises with the 30s:

| | Load | Reps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 15 | ~10, taken as 7 then 3–4 |
| 2 | 20 | 5 |
| 3 | **30 — the working weight** | 1–2 |

**That last rung is what makes this ramp different: it sits *at* the working load, not below it.** *"And
then I would maybe do a single or double with the working weight, since the total amount isn't that
high."* On a light isolation movement there is nothing to gain from approaching from underneath.

### A data error this exposed

The dumbbell rack in `exerciseHelpers.ts` was missing the **15s, 20s and 7.5s** — it ran 12.5 → 17.5 →
22.5 → 25. His example walks straight through the missing ones, and names 22.5 as the *rare* one: *"I'd go
up to twenty two point five if they had them, but most gyms don't, so I'll just say twenty."* Every load
snapped for a light dumbbell exercise was landing on a weight the rack doesn't have or skipping one it
does. Fixed.

## 16. Rep schemes: a four-week block, worked

The most detailed thing in this document, because it is a full worked block rather than a rule.

### The weeks have names

*"You have your base week, then your load week, then your peak week, and then your deload week."* His own
spreadsheet already labels its week pairs **BASE** and **LOAD**, so this is vocabulary he writes down.

He then walked a four-week example whose *fourth* week was the peak, with a 20 lb gain by the end of it.
Both are true at once if the deload is a fifth week most clients never see — C7 says below five sessions
a week there is no scheduled deload, so the block runs base, load, peak, peak and ends.

### The example: pressing compound, 50 lb dumbbells, opening at 8 reps

A "pressing compound" is *"a heavy dumbbell press, flat or incline, a Smith machine press, a barbell
bench, an incline barbell bench."*

| Week | | Tonnage |
|---|---|---|
| base | 3×8 @ 50 | 1200 |
| load | **2×8 @ 55, 1×8 @ 50** | 1280 |
| peak | 1×7 @ 60, 2×8 @ 55 | 1300 |
| peak | **1×8 @ 60, 1×8 @ 55, 1×10 @ 50** | 1420 |

*"A total of a twenty pound jump in your press ability by the end of the fourth week."*

### Three next weeks, and they are equivalent

This is the part that most resists being one formula. From 3×8 @ 50 he offers three week-twos:

| | Becomes | Tonnage rise |
|---|---|---|
| **1. More load** | 2×8 @ 55, 1×8 @ 50 | +7% |
| 2. More reps | 3×10 @ 50 | +25% |
| 3. More reps, front-loaded | 10, 8, 8 @ 50 | +8% |

> *"Why would you pick one over the other? There isn't really a reason at all. If you care more about load
> on the bar, I would pick number one. Number one is usually the one I would go with."*

**So the engine should not produce one answer.** It produces the small set of equivalent ones in his
preference order, and a coach takes the second if they prefer it. *"It's all about the relative intensity
and keeping the volume close enough that it's very close in terms of stress."*

Option 3 is easy to miss as a distinct lever — front-loading reps produces a descending scheme
deliberately, which is the same shape fatigue produces on its own (section 10). It reads as normal to the
client and still adds volume.

### Finishing a jump is not the same as jumping again

*"Instead of doing fifty-fives for one or two sets, I'm going to try to do all three sets with fifty-five.
Since you did one or two of them last time, that's a reasonable progression."*

That is `levelUp` — the lagging sets catch up to the weight the others already used. A `jumpLoad` here
would take the top set to 60 and skip the rung the stagger deliberately created. **Two different moves
that both look like "add load", and the smaller one comes first.**

### A rule of thumb, checked against the chart

*"Usually, if you can do fifty-fives for ten reps, you can do sixties for seven reps."*

At equal difficulty the table puts a 10-rep load at **0.88×** a 7-rep load, and that ratio is stable
across every rung of relative intensity (0.878–0.881 from 85% to 95%). So 60 for 7 pairs with about
**52.8** for 10 — meaning someone who can do 55 for 10 should manage rather more than 60 for 7.

**His heuristic is slightly conservative against the chart**, which is the safe direction. Worth recording
as one of the few places his anecdotal number and the table can be compared directly, and they agree to
within a couple of pounds.

### One thing he stated in passing that matters

*"You can still get stronger in the lower hypertrophy range by quite a bit."* Which is why this is filed
as a hypertrophy block that also builds strength, rather than a compromise between the two — and why the
20 lb gain above is not a surprise.

## 17. The strength block: a descending ladder

The mirror of section 16. Same four weeks, opposite direction, and a much bigger strength result.

*"This is something I would be doing if you were younger, of my age, serious — you were trying to get
strong, you wanted to get bigger as well, but you wanted to get strong."*

### Opening at 3×6 and walking down

| Week | Scheme | Total reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3×6 | 18 | — |
| 2 | 4×5 | **20** | +10 |
| 3 | 5×4 | **20** | +10 |
| 4 | 5×3 | **15** | +5 to +10 |

**Sets rise as reps fall, holding the rep total level — and then volume drops at the end.** The reason is
P7 restated with numbers: *"the same number of reps, but because there's only four of them each set,
they're a little bit more stimulative."* Holding the total constant is *already* an increase in stress,
and the added load is paid for out of that.

Sets climb to five and stop. C1's cap of four applies to the 6–15 rep range, so 5×4 and 5×3 don't violate
it — the cap and this ladder never overlap, which is worth noticing rather than assuming.

*"You could pretty much do this for anything other than, like, cleans or snatches."*

### Coming back up: the same ladder in reverse

> *"We're pretty much just gonna do what we just talked about in reverse. If we're doing five triples by
> the end of our last block and we want to transition back up to the six rep range, we start at five sets
> of three, then take away five or ten pounds and go for four sets of five or four sets of four. And then
> coming out of that we'll do five sets of four, and then probably four sets of six."*

| Week | Scheme | Total reps | Load |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5×3 | 15 | — |
| 2 | 4×4 | 16 | −5 to −10 |
| 3 | 5×4 | 20 | −5 to −10 |
| 4 | 4×6 | **24** | −5 to −10 |

The two ladders meet: one ends at 5×3 and the other begins there, so a strength block and its way out are
one continuous eight weeks.

**Both are recorded as data rather than derived from a rule**, because his ascending sequence is not a
simple increment — it holds four reps for two weeks and adds a set instead, then jumps straight to six.
Whatever produces that shape hasn't been stated, and a generator that guessed at it would be inventing.

*One ambiguity left visible:* he offered *"four sets of five or four sets of four"* for week two and then
*"five sets of four"* for week three — and 4×5 followed by 5×4 doesn't ascend, since the rep count goes
back down. Taking the 4×4 option makes the ladder monotone, so that's what's encoded.

### Why effort climbs anyway — the mechanism behind P10

> *"Note that because of this structure and the weight jumps, your RPE and/or reps in reserve will get
> closer to zero as you progress."*

This is what P10 had been missing. A block opens at RIR 3 and closes near RIR 0 **not because anyone
prescribes an RPE each week**, but because the load steps are deliberately smaller than the rep change
would warrant. Going down the ladder, load rises by less than the lost reps free up; coming back up, it
falls by less than the added reps cost. Either way the set gets closer to failure every week and nobody
has to write that down.

Checked against the table, on a 300 lb max:

| | Load | Relative intensity |
|---|---|---|
| A triple | 250 | 90% |
| Six reps, one 10 lb step off | 240 | **95%** |
| Six reps, the full 20 off | 230 | 90% — *and the block goes nowhere* |

**The under-compensation is the entire progression.** Take the "correct" amount of load off and effort
stays flat, which is why the step is 5–10 lb rather than the 20 the rep change would justify.

### Climbing in reps is the expensive option

The same block run the other way — opening at six and heading up into hypertrophy — is explicitly worse
for strength:

> *"Each time you do this you might be able to take a very small weight jump, perhaps ten or fifteen
> pounds throughout the block. Wouldn't be as much as it would be if you kept the reps the same or
> dropped them, but you'd still get a bit stronger."*

| Direction | Load gain across four weeks |
|---|---|
| Reps up (6 → 8) | **10–15 lb** |
| Reps down (6 → 3) | **25–30 lb** |

Roughly double, for the same four weeks. Worth stating plainly, because "add a rep" is the
cheapest-*looking* progression and it is the expensive one when strength is the goal.

Set against section 16's hypertrophy block, which produced a 20 lb gain while climbing from 8 reps
upward, this also gives the three block types a clean ordering: **descending strength ladder > hypertrophy
block > ascending rep block**, for strength specifically. And *"you can still get stronger in the lower
hypertrophy range by quite a bit"* is why the middle one is not a compromise.

## 18. Which lever, and why the floors exist

### One rep is a percentage, and at low reps it is a big one

This is the reason behind C8, stated from the stress side rather than as a list of exercises:

> *"The five rep range and below, you can't just add a rep, because the percentage increase is so high.
> If you're doing four reps and you try to add a rep next time, and you're already at RPE eight, that's a
> **twenty percent increase in stress in one session with a very heavy load, and you can very easily hurt
> yourself.** But it's easier just to add a little bit more weight and have the total stress
> significantly less with a similar amount of volume. Being able to use reps as a modality is a bit better
> in the rep range of six and up, and even more so from ten up, and definitely so fifteen to twenty —
> just because the percentage increase is so much smaller."*

| Reps | One rep costs | Lever |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | **33%** | load only |
| 4 | **25%** | load only |
| 5 | **20%** | load only |
| 6 | 16.7% | either |
| 10 | 10% | reps preferred |
| 15 | 6.7% | reps strongly preferred |
| 20 | 5% | reps strongly preferred |

**The rep lever isn't merely less useful at low reps — it's the dangerous one.** And this explains C8's
floors properly: the exercises allowed below six reps are exactly the ones where the *load* lever comes in
increments small enough to use instead.

It is also the same exchange rate as Model C, seen from the other end. There it priced a load jump in
reps; here it prices a rep in percent of the set. One arithmetic, two rules.

### The ascending hypertrophy block, 6 → 10

| Week | Scheme | Total reps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3×6 | 18 |
| 2 | **3×7** | 21 |
| 3 | 4×8 | 32 |
| 4 | 4×10 | **40** |

Load gain across the block: *"maybe ten pounds, definitely no more than twenty."*

**Volume more than doubles while load moves 10–20 lb** — the exact mirror of the descending ladder, where
volume fell from 20 reps to 15 and load moved 25–30. The same trade, seen from the other side.

### Exercises at home at both ends

*"You can do dumbbell presses and dumbbell rows and things like that at higher rep ranges as well. They
can also be used at lower ranges like that really well."*

So the C8 floor of 6 is a floor, not a home — these movements are equally correct at 15, which matters for
selection: a dumbbell press can fill a heavy compound slot or a mid-session accessory slot without
changing what it is.

## 19. The high-rep block, and the accessory load cap

*"For the twelve to fifteen rep range, it is much easier to use reps as a way to increase intensity — or
just keep the reps the same and keep adding load."*

### Two variations, both his

**A — never change the scheme.** *"You could just keep it three or four sets of ten the entire block and
add five pounds a week."*

| Week | | |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3×10 @ 40 | |
| 2 | 3×10 @ 45 | |
| 3 | 3×10 @ 50 | |
| 4 | 3×10 @ 55 | *"by the peak week you might miss it"* |

**And the condition attached to it is P10 stated for the client:** *"you'll probably be able to do it for
the other ones — **assuming you're not training too hard to failure right away in week one.**"* The whole
block works because week one leaves room. Open at RIR 3 and there are three weeks of runway; open at RIR 0
and there is none.

**B — convert one set at a time from 10 to 12.**

| Week | | Tonnage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3×10 @ 40 | 1200 |
| 2 | **1×12 @ 45**, 2×10 @ 40 | 1340 |
| 3 | 2×12 @ 45, 1×10 @ 40 | 1480 |
| 4 | 3×12 @ 45 | 1620 |

**This is the same stagger as the load jump, on the other axis.** Model C promotes sets to a new *weight*
one at a time and lets the leftovers catch up; this promotes them to a new *rep count* the same way. One
mechanic, two levers — and a set joining the promoted group takes the weight that group is already using,
*"the same weight that you did last week on the sets of twelve."*

*A tension worth recording:* he offers bumping the load on the converted set at the same time — *"maybe
with the set of twelve, you increase the weight a little bit"* — which moves two variables on one set and
sits awkwardly against C4. He said "maybe", so it is optional and off by default.

### The accessory load cap

> *"If we're talking accessories, which is pretty much the ten rep range and upwards, if you're gonna take
> a weight jump it should not be more than **five pounds per week** in most cases. If we're talking a
> cable exercise on a stack or a machine, you might be able to take ten — but **I wouldn't do fifteen**,
> and I definitely wouldn't try doing ten or fifteen **more than one week in a row**."*

| Equipment | Max weekly jump |
|---|---|
| Dumbbell, barbell | **5 lb** |
| Machine, cable stack | 10 lb, *and not two weeks running* |
| Anything | never 15 |

**That lands exactly on `stepForEquipment`** — 5 for dumbbells and barbells, 10 for machines and stacks.
The rule and the hardware agree, which is a good sign both are right: the cap is simply *one equipment
step a week, and no more.*

The second clause is a separate rule and a stricter one — a 10 lb stack jump may not repeat in consecutive
weeks, so a machine has to spend a week consolidating before it moves again.
