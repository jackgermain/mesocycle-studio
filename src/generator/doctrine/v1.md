# Progression doctrine — v1

Jack's programming rules, in his words, structured so an engine can run on them. Injected as context for
generation, and recorded by version against every draft so a decision stays explicable later.

Three tiers, and the difference matters:

- **Constraints** are never violated. Code enforces them; the model is not asked to respect them.
- **Principles** must trend the right way. Direction is fixed, magnitude is not.
- **Parameters** are defaults chosen to be adjusted once real output has been looked at.

---

## 1. Constraints

**C1 — Set cap.** In the 6–15 rep range, no more than **4 sets** on one exercise. 6×10 is "a ton" and
never written. 6×6 is still a lot. Observed practice sits at 2–3 sets; 4 is a ceiling approached, not a
target. *(Confirmed against ten real programs: 1,501 prescriptions at 3 sets, 1,400 at 2, only 84 at 4.)*

**C2 — Every working set is hard.** A set counts only if taken near failure: **RPE ≥ 7, or RIR ≤ 3**, which
is the same statement as **relative intensity ≥ 80%**. A set below that is not a working set.

**C3 — Hypertrophy rep range.** 6–30 reps. Below 6 is strength work, not this.

**C4 — One variable at a time.** Something must change every session, and only one thing. Load, reps, or
exercise variation — never two at once. *"We're thinking about this like a science experiment. We have
variables that are constant, and we have variables that change."*

**C5 — Only approved exercises.** Anything prescribed must exist in the library or the coach's own
additions. Enforced by validation, not by asking the model nicely.

---

## 2. Principles

**P1 — Stress rises every week.** The default direction is up, never "hold". Increase comes from one of
three levers: **a rep**, **a little load**, or **a variation of the same movement**.

**P2 — Small and predictable beats large and surprising.** The body should be able to anticipate the
increase. *"Controlled... somewhat predictable, but not so much that it causes something the body can't
completely expect."*

**P3 — Position in the session earns priority.** Earlier exercises get more available energy, so that is
where progression should land. Later exercises can hold.

**P4 — Novelty is itself a stimulus.** A new exercise carries an acute learning phase — more soreness,
more sensitivity — for a week or two. During it, less intensity is needed on top, because the variation
already spent the week's change. Corollary: a brand-new exercise starts lower on the intensity ramp.

**P5 — The session's total work output is what must rise.** Per-exercise changes serve that. This is a
session-level budget, not independent per-exercise progressions.

**P6 — Volume falls modestly as load rises — not proportionally.** Going 4×10 → 4×8 → 4×6 → 4×5 is
40 → 32 → 24 → 20 reps, and that drop is too steep. Some decline is correct and expected; that much is
not. Three things are true at once: sets may flex upward to protect volume, some decline is acceptable,
and part of it is corrected by feel.

**P7 — Reps near failure are worth more.** As a set approaches failure, each rep recruits more motor
units and is more stimulative. So raw rep totals across different intensities are not comparable — 20
reps at 78.8% is not half of 40 reps at 65.3%. This is what licenses P6's decline.

**P8 — Prilepin is a shape, not a budget.** The relationship it describes — as intensity climbs, total
reps must fall — transfers. Its numbers do not: it optimises for strength and bar speed, and its 65–75%
band prescribes 18 total reps against roughly 40 in real hypertrophy practice. Use the direction, ignore
the figures.

## 3. The two progression models

Both are real and both were given as worked examples. They differ in which variable moves.

### Model A — hold reps, walk intensity up

Four weeks of bench, 4×10 throughout, one step up the relative-intensity column each week:

| Week | Reps | Relative intensity | % of 1RM |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 85.0% | 61.8% |
| 2 | 10 | 87.5% | 63.5% |
| 3 | 10 | 90.0% | 65.3% |
| 4 | 10 | 92.5% | 67.3% |

**Rule: +1 row (2.5 percentage points of relative intensity) per week, reps constant.**

### Model B — hold intensity, walk reps down

Four weeks of bench at a constant 90% relative intensity, rep scheme descending:

| Week | Scheme | % of 1RM | Total reps |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4×10 | 65.3% | 40 |
| 2 | 4×8 | 72.0% | 32 |
| 3 | 4×6 | 76.5% | 24 |
| 4 | 4×5 | 78.8% | 20 |

**Rule: relative intensity constant, reps step down; the changing rep scheme is the variation.**
Subject to P6 — that volume drop is steeper than wanted and wants softening.

## 4. Parameters (defaults, to be tuned against output)

| Parameter | Default | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly relative-intensity step | **+2.5 pp** | One row of the table, per Model A |
| Starting relative intensity, new exercise | **85%** | Where Model A opens; leaves four weeks of ramp under the ceiling |
| Starting relative intensity, continuing exercise | *unspecified* | Learning phase already spent — open question |
| Top of the ramp | *unspecified* | Model A ends at 92.5% with 95/97.5/100 unused — open question |
| Sets, working range | **2–3** | Observed practice, under the C1 cap of 4 |
| Volume decay per intensity step | *unspecified* | P6 says "modest"; the number is not yet given |

## 5. Not yet specified

Recorded so nothing here is quietly invented:

- Where a **continuing** (non-novel) exercise starts on the ramp.
- The **ceiling** — whether the ramp runs into 95–100%, and what resets when it tops out.
- Whether that reset **is** the deload, and what a deload week actually looks like.
- The **volume budget per intensity band** in hypertrophy terms — Prilepin's column rewritten.
- **Exercise selection**: what picks lat pulldown over a row for a given person.
- **Session order**: whether there is a rule beyond "big things early, core late".
- **Swap triggers**: what takes an exercise out mid-block.
