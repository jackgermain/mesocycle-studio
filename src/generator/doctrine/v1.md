# Progression doctrine — v1

Jack's programming rules, in his words, structured so an engine can run on them. Injected as context for
generation, and recorded by version against every draft so a decision stays explicable later.

Three tiers, and the difference matters:

- **Constraints** are never violated. Code enforces them; the model is not asked to respect them.
- **Principles** must trend the right way. Direction is fixed, magnitude is not.
- **Parameters** are defaults chosen to be adjusted once real output has been looked at.

---

## 1. Constraints

**C1 — Set cap.** In the 6–15 rep range, no more than **4 sets** on one exercise. 6×10 is "a ton" and
never written. 6×6 is still a lot. Observed practice sits at 2–3 sets; 4 is a ceiling approached, not a
target. *(Confirmed against ten real programs: 1,501 prescriptions at 3 sets, 1,400 at 2, only 84 at 4.)*

**C2 — Every working set is hard.** A set counts only if taken near failure: **RPE ≥ 7, or RIR ≤ 3**, which
is the same statement as **relative intensity ≥ 80%**. A set below that is not a working set.

**C3 — Hypertrophy rep range.** 6–30 reps. Below 6 is strength work, not this.

**C4 — One variable at a time.** Something must change every session, and only one thing. Load, reps, or
exercise variation — never two at once. *"We're thinking about this like a science experiment. We have
variables that are constant, and we have variables that change."*

**C5 — Only approved exercises.** Anything prescribed must exist in the library or the coach's own
additions. Enforced by validation, not by asking the model nicely.

**C6 — The ceiling is a load cap, not a difficulty cap.** A block ramps until its last week before the
deload, and what stops it is **how much weight is on the bar**, not how hard the set feels. The cap is
**87.5% of 1RM**. *"If I were doing triples, I definitely would not be going at one hundred percent —
I'd go nothing more than an RPE 9 or 1 RIR... say we're doing sets of twelve, I don't think there's
anything wrong at all going to a 100% relative intensity set by the end of the mesocycle before a
deload, just because the total amount of weight on the bar would have to be significantly less, and
thus injury won't happen."* Both of those fall straight out of the one cap:

| Reps | Hardest legal rung | = % of 1RM | Next rung up would be |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 90% | 85.5% | 88.0% — over |
| 3 | **92.5% (RPE 9 / 1 RIR)** | 85.8% | 88.0% — over |
| 4 | 95% | 85.5% | 87.8% — over |
| 5 | 100% | 87.5% | *nothing above maximal* |
| 6–30 | **100%** | 85% and falling | — |

**Consequence: across the whole hypertrophy range of C3, the ceiling is simply 100%.** The cap only ever
binds at five reps and below, which is strength work. Injury risk is the reason the ceiling exists, and
it is a function of absolute load, which is why the engine can be permissive with high-rep work and must
not be with low-rep work.

**C7 — Below five sessions a week, there is no scheduled deload.** *"I definitely wouldn't deload anybody
who is training anything less than four times a week. Even four times a week, it's not really needed."*
This is the answer to why two real clients ran 41 and 47 weeks unbroken: not a deload hidden somewhere
the analysis missed — there wasn't one. Most of the roster trains 2–4×, so for most of the roster the
answer to "when do we deload" is **we don't**.

## 2. Principles

**P1 — Stress rises every week.** The default direction is up, never "hold". Increase comes from one of
three levers: **a rep**, **a little load**, or **a variation of the same movement**.

**P2 — Small and predictable beats large and surprising.** The body should be able to anticipate the
increase. *"Controlled... somewhat predictable, but not so much that it causes something the body can't
completely expect."*

**P3 — Position in the session decides *which lever*, not just who gets one.** Earlier exercises get more
available energy, so that is where progression should land — and specifically, **early exercises get the
load, late exercises get the reps**. *"In order to get stronger, you have to put more load on the bar.
But what you won't downplay from [adding reps] at all is hypertrophy... So this can be helpful for
exercises after your big heavy hitters, which are usually done earlier in the session."*

**P4 — Novelty is itself a stimulus.** A new exercise carries an acute learning phase — more soreness,
more sensitivity — for a week or two. During it, less intensity is needed on top, because the variation
already spent the week's change. Corollary: a brand-new exercise starts lower on the intensity ramp.

**P5 — The session's total work output is what must rise.** Per-exercise changes serve that. This is a
session-level budget, not independent per-exercise progressions.

**P6 — Volume falls modestly as load rises — not proportionally.** Going 4×10 → 4×8 → 4×6 → 4×5 is
40 → 32 → 24 → 20 reps, and that drop is too steep. Some decline is correct and expected; that much is
not. Three things are true at once: sets may flex upward to protect volume, some decline is acceptable,
and part of it is corrected by feel.

**P7 — Reps near failure are worth more.** As a set approaches failure, each rep recruits more motor
units and is more stimulative. So raw rep totals across different intensities are not comparable — 20
reps at 78.8% is not half of 40 reps at 65.3%. This is what licenses P6's decline.

**P8 — Prilepin is a shape, not a budget.** The relationship it describes — as intensity climbs, total
reps must fall — transfers. Its numbers do not: it optimises for strength and bar speed, and its 65–75%
band prescribes 18 total reps against roughly 40 in real hypertrophy practice. Use the direction, ignore
the figures.

**P9 — Pick the two ends, then fill the gaps.** This is the whole progression rule and it is worth
quoting exactly: *"First, when deciding to program, you need to decide where you're gonna start and
where you want to finish. And then you fill in the gaps. So if you've got four weeks, you want to have
appropriate progressions each week such that they're a similar size step forward."*

**The weekly step is an output, not a setting.** It falls out of the two ends and the length. The
previous version of this doctrine had it backwards — it fixed the step at 2.5 pp and derived the start
by counting back from the ceiling, which cannot express a four-week block that spans the full range.

**P10 — A block traverses the entire legal range.** *"I usually like to start blocks around RPE 7 or
RIR 3, and then end the block around RPE 9 or 1 rep in reserve, or even zero reps in reserve."* RIR 3 is
exactly the C2 working-set floor and RIR 0 is exactly the C6 ceiling, so a block is not a segment of the
ladder — it is the **whole ladder, floor to ceiling**, compressed into however many weeks there are.

That is what sets the step. Four weeks across the full span steps 80 → 87.5 → 92.5 → 100, which is
*precisely* the RIR 3 → 2 → 1 → 0 he described in the same breath, arrived at from the ladder alone. It
also fixes the maximum length of an unbroken ramp at **nine weeks** — nine rungs, one a week, P1
forbidding a repeat.

**P11 — The body's reference point is the most recent load, not a maximum.** This is the mechanism under
Model C, and it is why anchoring to last week is correct rather than merely convenient:

> *"Strength is a lot of it a central nervous system adaptation. When you go from thirty fives to
> forties, the percentage load increase is very high even if it's only five pounds. Your body doesn't
> recognize that necessarily. It recognizes what the most recent amount of load you had going through
> that particular vector and direction on your body is... Golgi tendon organs will send an inhibitory
> signal to the muscle fiber to not contract as hard, to protect itself, just because the load on the
> tendon is unfamiliar as in heavy."*

Three things follow directly:

1. **A new load is inhibited on first exposure.** The reps that come out of it are fewer than the
   arithmetic predicts — *"they'd probably only be able to do eight partly because of this."* So the
   first week at a new weight always lands on the band floor, however small the jump looks in pounds.
2. **The rep drop deliberately overpays.** 35 → 40 lb is 14.3%, which the exchange rate prices at 1.7
   reps off a set of twelve. He writes **eight** — a drop of four, more than double. The extra is paying
   for the inhibition, not for the work.
3. **The reference is per movement vector, not per exercise name.** Which is a caution about swapping a
   variation and assuming the load carries: what carries is what that tissue has recently seen.
4. **A detrained person is inhibited everywhere at once.** *"When you're detrained, your GTOs will
   massively limit force output. A lot of the very early gains that you make when somebody first starts
   lifting are central nervous system adaptations and the desensitisation of those GTOs."*

That fourth point has a consequence the engine must not get wrong. A beginner's load will climb fast for
the first weeks, and **it is not evidence the hypertrophy stimulus is well calibrated** — it is
inhibition lifting. So:

- Rapid early progress is expected and should not be read as "the program is working, push harder".
- Nor should it be extrapolated. The rate will fall off when the CNS adaptation is spent, and a plan
  built on the early slope will overshoot.
- P4's learning phase for a *new exercise* is the same mechanism in miniature. A detrained beginner is
  in that state on every exercise simultaneously, which is why their first block is a special case
  rather than a normal block with smaller numbers.

## 3. The three progression models

Both are real and both were given as worked examples. They differ in which variable moves.

### Model A — hold reps, walk intensity up

Four weeks of bench, 4×10 throughout, one step up the relative-intensity column each week:

| Week | Reps | Relative intensity | % of 1RM |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 85.0% | 61.8% |
| 2 | 10 | 87.5% | 63.5% |
| 3 | 10 | 90.0% | 65.3% |
| 4 | 10 | 92.5% | 67.3% |

**Rule: +1 row (2.5 percentage points of relative intensity) per week, reps constant.**

### Model B — walk the load up, walk the reps down

**This is a bridge block, not a hypertrophy block.** Jack's own classification: *"a really good
algorithm to have for a block that is in between a hypertrophy block and a strength block — it's sort of
the bridge in between, where you're transitioning away from the hypertrophy work into the strength
work."* It should not be the default for a general-population client who is not heading anywhere near a
strength phase.

His actual prescription, given in full and corrected from the earlier sketch of it:

| Week | Scheme | % of 1RM | Relative intensity | Total reps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3×10 | 65.3% | 90% | 30 |
| 2 | 3×8 | 72% | 90% | 24 |
| 3 | 4×6 | 76% | 90% | 24 |
| 4 | 4×5 | **83%** | **95%** | 20 |
| 4 *(alt)* | 5×5 | **81%** | 92.5% | 25 |

Three things here were wrong in the earlier record and matter:

1. **Sets are not constant.** They flex *upward* as reps fall — 3, 3, 4, then 4 or 5. This is P6's
   "sets may flex upward to protect volume", with numbers attached for the first time.
2. **The last week escalates.** Difficulty is held at 90% for three weeks and then pushed to 92.5–95%
   at the end. *"You could even make this a little bit higher... increase up to 81 or even 83 percent
   by the end of that same block."*
3. **The volume decline is far gentler than recorded.** 30 → 24 → 24 → 20, not 40 → 32 → 24 → 20. On the
   5×5 ending it is 30 → 24 → 24 → **25**, essentially flat after week 1. P6's "modest, not
   proportional" now has a shape: *one step down early, then hold*.

**The progression here is carried by load, not by difficulty.** *"In terms of relative load on your
joint, you're starting the block at 65% of your 1RM, and by the end you're pushing about 80%."* Which is
why this model needs a different fill rule from Model A.

### The two fill modes

Both models are "pick the ends and fill the gaps"; they differ in *which axis* is spaced evenly.

| | Model A — difficulty mode | Model B — load mode |
|---|---|---|
| Even steps in | relative intensity | % of 1RM |
| The other axis | load follows the table | difficulty follows the table |
| Reps | constant | descend |
| Said out loud as | "RIR 3, then 2, then 1, then 0" | "65% up to about 80% on the joint" |
| Use for | hypertrophy | bridging into strength |

Spacing % of 1RM evenly from 65.3% to 83% across four weeks and snapping each week through the table
returns **65.3 / 72 / 76.5 / 83.3** — against the 65.3 / 72 / 76 / 83 he gave from memory. The model
reproduces his own numbers to within rounding, which is the best evidence so far that the table and the
rule are both right.

*One artifact worth his eye:* the 5×5 @ 81% ending pulls week 2 down to 87.5% relative intensity while
weeks 1 and 3 sit at 90%, so difficulty **dips** mid-block even though load rises every week. The 4×5 @
83% ending does not do this. Whether that dip matters is not yet known.

### Model C — double progression, with the load jump staggered across sets

**This is the one he actually runs on a general-population client, and it never touches a 1RM.** Asked
directly what he looks at to write Carolyn's next session, the answer contained no maximum, no
percentage, and no table lookup — only last week's log.

> *"If I've got three sets of ten with the thirties and they move pretty well, I might say, okay, let's
> either do... two sets of eight with the thirty fives and then one set of eight with the thirties. That
> way, the load increase, even though it's not very small relatively in percentage, is slightly offsetted
> by the volume being ever so slightly lower."*

| Week | Prescription | Tonnage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3×10 @ 30 | 900 |
| 2 | **2×8 @ 35, 1×8 @ 30** | 800 |
| 3 | 2×9 @ 35, 1×9 @ 30 | 900 |
| 4 | 2×10 @ 35, 1×10 @ 30 | 1000 |

**The stagger is the trick worth having.** Moving two of three sets to the next dumbbell is an *effective*
3.3 lb jump out of a rack that only has 5 lb steps — a finer increment than the equipment offers. The
set left behind is what next week promotes.

**The load jump costs a week of tonnage and takes two weeks to earn back.** That dip at week 2 is not an
error; P7 licenses it, because eight reps with the 35s sits closer to failure than ten with the 30s.

**The rep band is what gates the jump.** *"I may, with a client, keep the reps above eight reps at all
times regardless of whether I make a weight jump."* Reps float 8→12; hitting 12 is what earns the load
move; the jump drops the whole exercise — promoted sets and left-behind set alike — back to 8.

### Model C at the top of the band

3×12 with the 35s, and the last set is a fight. What he writes:

| | Prescription | Tonnage |
|---|---|---|
| before | 3×12 @ 35 | 1260 |
| after | **1×8 @ 40, 1×12 @ 35, 1×10 @ 35** | 1090 |

Note what is *different* from the first jump: only **one** set is promoted rather than two, and the sets
left behind **keep their reps** instead of dropping to the floor with it. Resetting all three would have
given 1×8 @ 40 + 2×8 @ 35 = 880, a 30% collapse in a single week. Holding them costs ~13%.

So the two examples disagree about the sets left behind, and Jack said so himself: *"you don't literally
have to do that, but I'm playing around with the reps and the relative intensities."* `holdRemainder`
defaults to holding, because it protects P5 and because this example is the more recent and more
detailed one — but the rule that picks between them is not known. The code also produces 2×12 where he
wrote 12 and 10; that last set is the "playing around", not a rule.

### The exchange rate between the two levers

This is what makes load and reps comparable *without* a 1RM, and it is arithmetic he stated outright:
*"if I just add a rep week over week with the same weight, you're increasing the total workload or stress
by ten percent each set."* One rep on a set of ten is 1/10 of the set.

| Load jump | Cost | = how many reps, at 10 reps |
|---|---|---|
| 30 → 35 lb | 16.7% | 1.7 |
| 15 → 20 lb | **33.3%** | 3.3 |
| 100 → 110 lb machine | 10.0% | 1.0 |

Which is why *"thirty to thirty five is a relatively small jump compared to going from fifteen pounds to
twenty pounds."* **The rack's step is fixed; its relative cost is not, and it is punishing at the light
end** — exactly where general-population accessory work lives. That is the real argument for the
stagger, and for preferring the rep lever late in a session.

### The effort scale, on the same ladder

RPE and RIR are the same axis as relative intensity, and C2 pins both ends of it: 0 RIR is by definition
maximal for that rep count, and RIR 3 / RPE 7 *is* the 80% working-set floor. Three reps in reserve
across twenty points is 6.67 pp each, snapped to real rungs:

| RIR | RPE | Relative intensity |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 10 | 100% |
| 1 | 9 | 92.5% |
| 2 | 8 | 87.5% |
| 3 | 7 | 80% — the floor |
| 4+ | ≤6 | not a working set |

This is what lets a client's logged RPE be read back as a position on the ramp, and what makes "RPE 9"
and "92.5%" the same instruction rather than two competing prescriptions.

## 4. Parameters (defaults, to be tuned against output)

| Parameter | Default | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Where a block opens | **80% RI = RIR 3 = RPE 7** | P10; the C2 floor, stated directly |
| Where a block closes | **92.5–100% RI = RIR 1–0** | P10, capped by C6 |
| Weekly step | **derived** | (start → end) ÷ (weeks − 1), per P9. Not a setting. |
| Longest unbroken ramp | **9 weeks** | Nine rungs floor-to-ceiling, one a week, P1 forbidding a repeat |
| Absolute load cap | **87.5% of 1RM** | C6; reproduces both of Jack's stated ceilings |
| Sets, working range | **2–3**, flexing to 4–5 as reps fall | Observed practice and Model B's 3/3/4/4–5 |
| Volume shape across a block | **one step down, then hold** | Model B: 30 → 24 → 24 → 20–25 |
| Starting relative intensity, new exercise | *unspecified* | P4 says lower — but 80% is already the floor, so "lower" must mean load, not difficulty |
| Rep band (Model C) | **8–12** | "keep the reps above eight reps at all times regardless of whether I make a weight jump" |
| Sets promoted on a load jump | **all but one** | Carolyn's 2-of-3; yields a finer increment than the rack has |
| Reps after a load jump | **the band floor** | The whole exercise resets, promoted sets and left-behind set alike |

## 5. Not yet specified

Recorded so nothing here is quietly invented:

- ~~Whether the **1RM is estimated and stored**.~~ **Answered: it is neither estimated nor stored.** The
  input to next week is last week's log. Model C runs entirely on it, and the relative intensity table
  becomes a *check* on the answer and an *explanation* of it rather than a calculator that produces it.
  Models A and B still speak in %1RM, so they remain coach-facing tools for someone who has a number,
  not the default path.
- **What decides between adding a rep and jumping the load** on a given week. *"It depends on the goal of
  the client, period."* P3 gives the positional half — early exercises take load, late ones take reps —
  but not the rest. Right now the lever is a caller's argument with no rule behind it.
- **Whether the rep band is per-exercise.** 8–12 was given for Carolyn's dumbbell bench. Nothing says
  whether a leg press or a lateral raise floats in the same band.
- **How a block longer than nine weeks works.** P10 fixes the span at floor-to-ceiling and P1 forbids a
  repeated rung, so nine weeks is a hard maximum for one ramp. The presets go to 24 weeks and two real
  clients ran 41 and 47 weeks unbroken. Long blocks must contain internal resets; `fillRamp()` returns
  undefined rather than inventing one.
- ~~**What a deload week actually is.**~~ **Mostly answered by C7:** below 5×/week there is no scheduled
  deload at all, which covers nearly the whole roster. What a deload contains *for someone training 5×+*
  is still unknown, but it is now an edge case rather than a hole in the middle.
- **What decides how many sets get promoted on a jump, and whether the rest hold their reps.** Two
  worked examples, two different answers (2-of-3 with everything reset; 1-of-3 with the rest held).
- **P4 versus P10.** P4 says a new exercise starts lower on the ramp; P10 says every block starts at the
  floor. Both cannot be about the same axis. Most likely P4 means a lower *load* at the same RIR, which
  would be automatic and need no rule — but that is a guess.
- **When to use Model A versus Model B** for a given person, given that Model B is explicitly a bridge
  into strength work rather than a hypertrophy progression.
- The **volume budget per intensity band** in hypertrophy terms — Prilepin's column rewritten.
- **Exercise selection**: what picks lat pulldown over a row for a given person.
- **Session order**: whether there is a rule beyond "big things early, core late".
- **Swap triggers**: what takes an exercise out mid-block.

## 6. Where the textbook sits (CSCS, ch. 17 & 21)

Pulled from *Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning*, chapter 17. Recorded because the
divergences are deliberate and should not be quietly "corrected" by anyone reading this later.

| | CSCS, hypertrophy | This practice |
|---|---|---|
| Load | **67–85% 1RM** (table 17.9) | ~65% at ten reps, 90% RI — *below the floor* |
| Reps | **6–12** (17.9, 17.11) | 10–20 routinely, occasionally 30 |
| Sets | **3–6** (17.11) | **2–3**, capped at 4 |
| Rest | **30 s – 1.5 min** (17.12) | 1:30–2:30 in the real programs |
| Frequency | beginner 2–3, intermediate 3–4, advanced 4–7 (17.4) | 2–4 |

**The divergence is consistent in one direction: lighter, longer sets, fewer of them, more rest.** That
is a defensible read of the population — CSCS is written for athletes, and these are middle-aged
general-population clients where joint cost and session tolerance matter more than squeezing the last
percent of hypertrophic stimulus. It is recorded here as a choice, not an oversight.

Where they agree, and which is therefore safe to encode as shared ground:

- **Exercise order**: power first, then multi-joint / large muscle, then single-joint / small muscle
  assistance. "Power exercises require the highest level of skill and concentration of all the exercises
  and are most affected by fatigue." This matches P3 and matches the ten real programs, which open with a
  squat or pull pattern and finish with core.
- **Sets rise with training age**: "It is appropriate for an athlete to perform only one or two sets as a
  beginner and to add sets as he or she becomes better trained." Sets are a function of what someone can
  tolerate, not a fixed prescription.
- **Load and reps are inversely related**, which is the whole basis of the relative intensity table.

### The load increment is not constant, and the table already knows it

One rung of relative intensity (2.5 percentage points), expressed as load:

| Reps | Δ %1RM per rung | On a 300 lb 1RM |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 2.5 pp | 7.5 lb |
| 10 | 2.0 pp | 6.0 lb |
| 20 | 1.0 pp | 3.0 lb |

Across the full 80→100% ladder: 18.5 pp of 1RM at three reps, 14.5 at ten, 9.0 at twenty.

So a fixed "+5 lb a week" rule is wrong at both ends. **The engine works in relative intensity and never
in pounds**, then snaps the result to what the equipment can actually do — `stepForEquipment` already
gives 5 lb for dumbbells and barbells, 10 for machines and cable stacks. A computed 82.4 lb has to become
an 85 lb dumbbell, because that is what exists on the rack.

## 7. Building week one

Everything above is week two onward — given last week's log, write next week. This is the other half.

### The split

**Full body, every day, with a rotating lead.** *"I would definitely give them a full body split. On the
first day you're starting with a chest exercise, the second day a back exercise, and the third day a leg
exercise. But each day you still do a little bit of full body."*

### The session shape

Top down, by importance. *"What's the most important? Compounds. Bang for your buck."*

| Slot | What goes there |
|---|---|
| 1 | The day's lead compound — chest / back / legs by day |
| 2 | A second compound, **a different body part from the lead** |
| 3 … n−2 | Accessories, "smaller body parts" |
| last 2 | *"Really small body parts, like abs or forearms or calves"* |

**No more compounds after slot 2.** *"You don't really need much more compound lifting unless you're
really trying to lift like a bodybuilder, and this specific person and demographic doesn't really need
that."*

### The lead exercise, per day

| Day | Lead | Candidates |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chest | Dumbbell or barbell press of some type — **or a push-up if they aren't strong**, and progress that |
| 2 | Back | A pull-up variation (assisted / bodyweight / pulldown) **if they want to work toward a pull-up**; otherwise **a row that is not chest supported**, or a hex bar deadlift |
| 3 | Legs | A squat |

Note both conditionals: the lead is chosen against the *person*, not the slot. Someone who can't press
gets a push-up as their week-one chest compound and the progression runs on that.

### The back taxonomy

Stated outright, and unusually tidy. *"With back exercises, there's only two types. There's vertical
pulling and horizontal pulling, and then your spinal erectors having to resist spinal flexion, which you
can take out of it if it's chest supported. Those are the only three types of back exercises you can
do."*

So **erector loading is a flag on a movement, not a pattern of its own.** A bent-over row and a
chest-supported row are the same horizontal pull; they differ only in whether the erectors work. Which is
exactly why he specifies "not chest supported" when he wants that stimulus, and why the day-two lead
carries a `wantsErectors` flag.

### The second slot

His stated rule is only "not the same body part as the lead." His worked week resolves the rest:

| Day | Lead | Second | His reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | incline dumbbell press | vertical pull | different body part |
| 2 | unsupported row | **flat** press | a second, different press variation |
| 3 | squat | vertical pull | *"we haven't done one since day one"* |

So: **push day takes a pull, pull day takes a push, leg day takes whatever has gone longest untrained**
— and within the winning category, the sub-pattern longest unscheduled, which is what steers day one
onto the *vertical* pull since the horizontal one is already day two's lead.

**One inference, flagged as such.** Day two takes a second horizontal press rather than the untouched
overhead press. With vertical pushing in the pool, the planner disagrees with him on two of six compound
slots; with it excluded, it reproduces his week exactly. That is a strong hint that overhead pressing is
deliberately absent for a middle-aged general-population client — but it is an inference from one
example, not a stated rule, and it is `DEFAULT_EXCLUDED` in the code purely so the default matches
observed practice.

### Exercises per session

He said "somewhere between nine and twelve", and *"if you're not planning on supersetting any exercises
together, which wouldn't happen until the later half of the workout anyway."*

His own ten programs say nine-to-twelve is the **two-day** number. The count falls as frequency rises:

| Days/week | Mean exercises per session | Clients |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | **9.8** | Carolyn, Marco, Teressa |
| 3 | **8.4** | Arish, Vanessa, Wasiqua, Jing |
| 4 | **6.8** | Bella, Colin |

Which is the sensible reading — fewer sessions, more work in each. Defaults are set from the data rather
than the quote.
